# A. OFFICIAL DOCUMENTATION  
The eSakshya app is mandated by the new BNSS (Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita) for recording searches/seizures.  For example, the *Maharashtra Government’s e-Sakshya Rules, 2025* (Notification, Home Dept., Govt. of Maharashtra, 28 Nov 2025) define *“Sakshya”* as any evidence (video, photographs of witnesses or officers) collected via the eSakshya app, and require that **every** investigating officer “record all video and photo evidence as required under sections 105, 173, 176, 180, 185 and 497 of the Sanhita through the eSakshya Mobile Application”.  These rules further mandate that each recording generate a secure “eSakshya packet” with a unique 16-digit SID, timestamps, geolocation and a hash to ensure integrity, link the SID to the FIR/GD via CCTNS, and upload the packet to immutable cloud storage for forwarding to the Magistrate.  

The Government of India has also issued guidance. A PIB press release (MoS Home Affairs, written reply, 5 Aug 2025) notes that eSakshya “enables lawful, scientific and tamper-proof collection, preservation and electronic submission of digital evidence”.  A later Rajya Sabha reply (MHA, 11 Feb 2026) confirmed that NIC rolled out eSakshya nationwide (used in ~15,900 police stations across 35 States/UTs), and that NCRB has developed CCTNS software patches to support Sections 105 (and related provisions).  (That reply explains eSakshya “facilitates capture, storage and retrieval of evidence through videography and photography” in compliance with the new laws.) 

State-level directives are emerging.  In Kerala, a High Court order (26 July 2025) directed the police to equip officers with eSakshya for all crimes (7+ years).  In Uttar Pradesh, the Allahabad HC (in *Shadab v State of U.P.*, Jan 2026) specifically directed the UP DGP to issue an SOP mandating live audio-video recording of searches via eSakshya.  (The UP high court also noted that the police must upload such recordings via the eSakshya portal under Rule 18 of the UP BNSS Rules.)  

**Sources:** Maharashtra e-Sakshya Rules 2025 (Govt. Gazette); MHA/PIB press releases; High Court orders and circulars (Kerala HC, 26 Jul 2025; Allahabad HC *Shadab* Jan 2026). 

# B. TECHNICAL BEHAVIOUR — WHAT THE APP PRODUCES  
eSakshya is designed as a *mobile app + web portal* for police evidence collection.  An investigating officer uses the app to record each search/seizure: it captures **video clips** (with optional photos of items or witnesses) and **audio** as needed.  In Maharashtra’s rules, each “Sakshya” package contains the recorded video(s) of the search, photos of seized items, and officer’s photo with fingerprint (“verification selfie”), etc. Each Sakshya is sealed into a *secure packet* with a **unique 16-digit Sakshya ID (SID)**, timestamps for start/end of recording, and precise GPS location.  The rules explicitly require each packet’s contents (i.e. video and files) to carry a **cryptographic hash** to ensure integrity. These hashes are stored with the packet in “immutable storage” (a government cloud), preserving evidence integrity. The officer must *link* this SID to the corresponding FIR/GD via the CCTNS system.  

Each video clip is reportedly limited in duration (e.g. NIC indicates a per-clip cap of **10 minutes**) and multiple clips may be recorded per case as needed. Still photos (of the scene or items) can be captured as part of the package. The app automatically stamps each clip/photo with date, time and GPS coordinates, and the recording officer’s identity (via their police login).  After the event, the app uploads the Sakshya packet (video, photos, metadata, officer’s details) to the central cloud. Under the rules, uploading constitutes forwarding the evidence to the Magistrate. The packet is viewable to authorized users: courts can access Sakshya via the ICJS/CIS portal (a “Sakshya portal” for courts).  

Notably, eSakshya **computes and binds a hash** for the recording packet (video + attached data) to guarantee it is tamper-proof.  The Maharashtra rules also require that the investigating officer generate a certificate under Section 63(4)(c) of the new Sakshya Act (Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam) via the app, which is e-signed and linked to the evidence packet. In practice, this means each recording is time-stamped, geo-tagged, officer-stamped, and cryptographically sealed.  (No evidence was found of any *printout* or display of the hash to the officer or court – it remains internal to the packet.)  

**Sources:** Maharashtra e-Sakshya Rules 2025 (detailed technical requirements); NIC/PIB descriptions. 

# C. THE DECISIVE QUESTION — POST-SEIZURE CUSTODY  
No official source documents any post-seizure inventory tracking in eSakshya.  The app and its portal focus solely on **capturing and uploading audiovisual evidence at the scene** of search/seizure. The eSakshya rules and manuals make no mention of handling the physical seized items thereafter.  In particular, we found **no documented eSakshya function for tracking transfer of seized items into malkhana (property room), to forensic lab, or production to court**. All mentions of property custody in the CCTNS context are in the separate “property register” modules (see next section), not part of eSakshya. 

Therefore, **eSakshya does not itself track an exhibit’s later chain-of-custody** (e.g. handing over to malkhana, FSL, or return/disposal).  No custody-transfer or exhibit-movement features are described in any SOP, user manual or rule found.  Its role ends once the video/photos are uploaded; further custody is expected to follow conventional malkhana procedures. 

**Sources:** Maharashtra e-Sakshya Rules and other guidance (silent on post-seizure custody); NCRB/CCTNS specifications (see D). *No document was found that eSakshya tracks item transfers after recording.* 

# D. DOES CCTNS ALREADY DO THIS?  
Yes. The Crime & Criminal Tracking Network (CCTNS/ICJS) **includes property/case-property modules** for evidentiary items, though not under “eSakshya.”  CCTNS’s standard Investigation Module has a *“Property Register Service”*, including an “Unclaimed/Abandoned Property Register Service”.  These allow police to log seized items and match them against lost/stolen property databases. For example, Tamil Nadu’s CCTNS RFP explicitly defines an **“Unclaimed/Abandon Property Register Service”** to record unclaimed items and match them to lost/stolen registers. NCRB’s central specifications similarly list a “Property Register Service” under Investigations. The Jharkhand CCTNS tender even required tracking the *status* of case property (e.g. “released/transferred to other PS/Lab/testing”) within the system. 

Thus, core CCTNS/ICJS does have modules for managing seized-property registers and transfers.  However, these have generally not been integrated with eSakshya recordings: they do not automatically associate the video evidence with the property registration or custody chain. No separate *“malkhana management”* tool appears to be used in ICJS beyond these registers. 

Separately, some States have procured stand-alone malkhana systems.  For instance, Bihar’s Buxar district police launched an **“e-Maalkhana”** app (July 2025) to digitize evidence-room inventory: it barcodes and photographs every seized item, recording case details and storage location. Delhi Police has piloted a similar QR-code e-malkhana system (News reports). These are **outside** CCTNS/ICJS. 

**Sources:** CCTNS RFPs and module lists.  State news: Buxar (Bihar) e-Maalkhana initiative. (No official central document; evidence is drawn from technical RFPs and news.) 

# E. INTEGRATION SURFACE  
eSakshya is integrated into CCTNS/ICJS via internal (police-judicial) networks, not by public APIs.  Under the rules, once an eSakshya recording is made, its unique SID is *linked* to the FIR/GD in CCTNS. The evidence packet is then uploaded to a NIC-managed cloud and made accessible to the courts.  Specifically, Maharashtra’s rules direct that uploaded Sakshya packets are forwarded to the Magistrate as per law, and “courts can view and manage all Sakshya…in the CIS application/Sakshya portal on ICJS”. In practice, this means the courts’ Case Information System (CIS) within ICJS has a portal for accessing eSakshya videos. 

We found **no public API** or third-party interface exposed. The system appears closed: only authorized police and judicial users (through CCTNS/ICJS) can upload or fetch Sakshya packets. There is no documentation of any external forensic tool retrieving or verifying an eSakshya file independently. The only linkage is that after upload, eSakshya forms part of the ICJS data (via the SID and linkage to FIR), and courts/judges use the ICJS portal to view it. 

**Sources:** Maharashtra e-Sakshya Rules (require linking SID to CCTNS, upload to Magistrate, and court CIS portal). No mention was found of public APIs – the integration is implicitly internal (police network and NIC cloud).

# G. JUDICIAL TREATMENT  
Multiple High Courts have already addressed eSakshya/Section 105. Notable judgments include:  

- *Shadab v. State of U.P.* (Allahabad HC, 05 Jan 2026): The court granted bail after noting that police **did not videograph a seizure of 40 motorcycles** as mandated by Section 105 BNSS.  It held that failure to record (and upload via eSakshya) “showed not only negligence but also arbitrariness” in the prosecution. The court directed the UP DGP to issue a detailed SOP mandating audio-video recording of all recoveries on the e-Sakshya portal, and warned of disciplinary action for officers who fail to comply.  
- *Mani Roy v. State of H.P.* (H.P. HC, 27 May 2025): The petitioner argued that a supplementary statement recorded on eSakshya was inadmissible because it lacked the certificate required under Section 63(c) of the Sakshya Act. The HP High Court accepted this objection, holding **that any eSakshya recording without the prescribed certificate (63(c)) is not admissible in evidence**. It granted bail accordingly.  
- *Suresh v. State of Kerala* (Kerala HC, 26 July 2025): A division bench set aside a murder conviction due to “irresponsible” crime-scene documentation.  The court emphasized that BNSS now **“obligates”** police to videotape every search and seizure and upload it immediately. It noted that failure to record the scene (and reliance only on a sketch and mahazar) justified acquittal. The court also **directed Kerala police to train and equip all officers to use eSakshya** or equivalent platforms for evidence recording.  

We found no reported Supreme Court decisions yet on eSakshya. (These are recent high court rulings interpreting BNSS Section 105 and eSakshya’s role.) Each case is cited above with court and date. 

**Sources:** Allahabad HC (*Shadab* 5 Jan 2026); H.P. HC (*Mani Roy* 27 May 2025); Kerala HC (*Suresh* 26 Jul 2025).
